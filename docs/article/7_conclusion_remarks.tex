\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We studied shortcut-aware reasoning training, a fundamental problem in language-model fine-tuning where shortcut samples reduce training loss yet collapse generalization the moment numbers, constraints, or phrasing shift. The takeaway is that \textbf{shortcut suppression factors into two orthogonal operations---ranking samples by gradient geometry, and projecting away only the directional component that genuinely opposes the validation gradient---and any correction that operates on magnitude alone (scalar reweighting) or on sample identity alone (data filtering) leaves the second operation undone}. Our framework \textbf{SART} instantiates this view as gradient-structure detection followed by conflict-only PCGrad-style surgery, requiring no shortcut-group labels and no multi-environment annotation. Across three synthetic reasoning benchmarks and a Qwen3-8B / GSM8K controlled-injection pilot, SART is the strongest method on average ($+12.0$\,pp accuracy and $+5.5$\,pp robustness over the best baseline) and the only one that exceeds 0.79 perturbed-accuracy on the confounder-laden causal-reasoning split, where surface-level filters and worst-group reweighting all fail. SART has three limitations: it treats the small validation gradient $\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}}$ as ground truth and reinforces shortcuts whenever $\mathcal{V}$ is itself contaminated; the answer-token suppression term is incompatible with autoregressive generation at large scale, where it must be restricted to numeric answer tokens; the directional gate, when implemented at batch granularity in the LLM regime, is masked by gradient averaging and requires per-sample gating to fire reliably. We plan to address all three through (i) counterfactual probes for $\mathcal{V}$ construction, (ii) numeric-only answer suppression, and (iii) per-sample conflict gating with Johnson--Lindenstrauss-sketched gradients to keep the per-sample bookkeeping tractable.
