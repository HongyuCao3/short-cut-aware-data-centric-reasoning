\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate SART on three synthetic reasoning benchmarks with controlled shortcut injection, comparing against eleven baselines spanning data-centric, loss-based, distributionally robust, and invariant learning approaches. We then transfer the method to Qwen3-8B / GSM8K under matched controlled injection.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}

\paragraph{Model and Training.} We use a GPT-style transformer (4 layers, $d_{\mathrm{model}}{=}256$, 8 heads, $d_{\mathrm{ff}}{=}1024$, ${\approx}$3.2M params) trained for 40 epochs with AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019decoupled} (lr $1{\times}10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, batch size 64, cosine annealing). SART hyperparameters ($\alpha{=}\beta{=}1.0$, $\tau_A{=}0.3$, $\tau_R{=}0.5$, $\gamma{=}1.0$, $\rho{=}0.3$, warmup $K{=}8$) are selected via an 80-configuration grid search (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_sensitivity}).

\paragraph{Datasets.} We construct three synthetic benchmarks with controlled shortcut injection: 2{,}000 train / 500 val / 1{,}000 test samples each, with training data 70\% shortcut-rule and 30\% true-rule (val/test exclusively follow the true rule; test split evenly into \emph{clean} and \emph{perturbed} by whether the shortcut agrees with the correct answer). \textbf{Math-Arithmetic}: classify $a{+}b{\geq}10$---shortcut $a{\geq}5$, true rule evaluates the sum. \textbf{Financial-Analysis}: regulatory compliance from four features---shortcut revenue${\geq}5$, true rule margin${\geq}5$ and debt${<}5$ (multi-feature conjunction). \textbf{Causal-Reasoning}: causal-direction inference with a confounder---shortcut correlation${\geq}5$, true rule $x{\geq}5$ and $z{<}3$ (requires confounder adjustment).

\paragraph{Baselines.} We compare against eleven methods spanning four families: \emph{loss / inference-time}---\textbf{SFT}, \textbf{Self-Consistency}~\cite{wang2023selfconsistency} (majority vote over 5 samples, $T{=}0.8$), \textbf{Focal Loss}~\cite{lin2018focallossdenseobject}, and \textbf{JTT}~\cite{liu2021justtraintwiceimproving} (upweight error-prone samples); \emph{data-centric}---\textbf{Data Filtering} (drop samples with post-warmup confidence $>0.90$) and \textbf{Influence Filtering}~\cite{koh2020understandingblackboxpredictionsinfluence}; \emph{invariance / worst-group}---\textbf{Group DRO}$^\dagger$~\cite{sagawa2020distributionallyrobustneuralnetworks}, \textbf{IRM}$^\ddagger$~\cite{arjovsky2020invariantriskminimization}, \textbf{V-REx}$^\ddagger$~\cite{krueger2021outofdistributiongeneralizationriskextrapolation}, \textbf{Fishr}$^\ddagger$~\cite{rame2022fishrinvariantgradientvariances}; and \emph{failure-mining}---\textbf{LfF}~\cite{nam2020learningfailuretrainingdebiased}. $^\dagger$ requires group annotations, $^\ddagger$ requires multiple environments.

\paragraph{Metrics.} All metrics are computed on held-out samples drawn from the true-rule distribution. \textbf{Clean Accuracy} ($\uparrow$) is token-level top-1 accuracy on the clean split. \textbf{Robustness} ($\uparrow$) is absolute accuracy on the perturbed split, where the shortcut feature contradicts the correct label---a model that has truly learned the rule should be unaffected, a shortcut-reliant model flips. \textbf{Reasoning Consistency} ($\uparrow$) is the fraction of test examples whose greedily generated intermediate tokens exactly match the gold chain up to the separator (protocol in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_algorithm}). \textbf{Shortcut Detection F1} ($\uparrow$) is the best binary F1 over a 50-threshold sweep of each method's per-sample score---an oracle upper bound on detection quality. The ablation table additionally reports \textbf{Gradient Alignment} $|\cos(\mathbf{g}_s,\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}})|$ over a held-out subset of up to 100 samples; values near $0$ indicate updates neither systematically aligned with nor opposed to the validation-improving direction.

\textbf{Findings.}
Table~\ref{tab:main_results} shows SART reaches \textbf{80.7\%} accuracy and \textbf{54.8\%} robustness, surpassing the strongest baseline (Influence Filtering: 76.0\%/47.7\%) by \textbf{+4.7\,pp} accuracy and \textbf{+7.1\,pp} robustness, and improving over SFT by \textbf{+11.9\,pp} accuracy and \textbf{+35.4\,pp} robustness---without group annotations (Group DRO) or multiple training environments (IRM, V-REx, Fishr). The underlying driver is that SART operates on per-sample gradient geometry, not on the loss values that loss-shaping baselines see and not on the sample identities that filtering baselines see; geometry is the only signal that distinguishes a shortcut sample's harmful direction from its still-aligned residual. The per-dataset robustness block in Table~\ref{tab:main_results} further shows that SART wins decisively on Causal-Reasoning (79.5, $+10.3$\,pp over Influence Filtering) but trails on single-feature Math (Influence Filtering 50.4 vs.\ SART 41.9) and on conjunction-rule Financial where Data Filtering's confidence threshold catches the shortcut directly (68.4 vs.\ SART 43.0). There are two possible reasons: 1) on single-feature shortcuts a confidence-threshold filter is already nearly optimal because the shortcut is the high-confidence head of the loss distribution, leaving little room for gradient-geometry to add; 2) on confounder-induced shortcuts, the shortcut and the target both produce confident, low-loss training examples that hard filters cannot separate, but their per-sample gradients diverge geometrically, so the conflict gate fires reliably and the projection cancels exactly the harmful direction. The net effect is that \uline{SART outperforms every baseline on average robustness without any shortcut-group or environment annotation, with the largest gains concentrated on the confounder-laden split where surface-level filters cannot separate shortcut from target.}

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:exp_ablation}

\textbf{Ablation Study.} This experiment aims to answer: \textbf{Is the conflict-gated directional projection the load-bearing primitive in SART, and do the two simpler corrections our method-selection argument rejects---scalar reweighting on the same \textsc{ShortcutScore} (magnitude-only) and answer-token suppression without projection (gate-free)---recover the same gain?}

We compare SART against variants that remove or replace one component at a time: \textbf{SART-w/o-projection} disables the conflict-only projection and keeps only the answer-token suppression; \textbf{SART-w/o-suppress} sets $\rho{=}0$ and keeps only the conflict-only projection; \textbf{SART-rwt} replaces directional surgery with scalar exponential reweighting $w(s){=}\exp(-\lambda S(s))$ at $\lambda{=}3.0$, holding the detection step fixed.

\textbf{Findings.}
Figure~\ref{fig:analysis}c shows that conflict-only projection is the load-bearing component: removing it (\textbf{-w/o-projection}) collapses robustness from 54.8 to 18.3\,pp ($-36.5$\,pp), while removing the answer-token suppression (\textbf{-w/o-suppress}, $\rho{=}0$) costs only $-3.9$\,pp robustness and $-1.1$\,pp accuracy. The mean absolute gradient alignment $|\cos(\mathbf{g}_s,\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}})|$ (defined in \S\ref{sec:exp_setup}) tracks the same ordering---SFT $0.08$, SART-rwt $0.06$, w/o-projection $0.07$, w/o-suppress $0.04$, SART (Full) $\mathbf{0.03}$---and the standalone detection F1 lies in the narrow band $0.82$--$0.85$ across all variants that use \textsc{ShortcutScore}, decoupling detection accuracy from downstream robustness. The underlying driver is that the conflict-gated projection is the single mechanism that converts a shortcut-flagged sample into a still-usable update, by canceling its anti-aligned direction without erasing its residual; the answer-token suppression amplifies this on tasks with token-localized shortcuts (Causal) but is not load-bearing on average. Another interesting observation is that scalar reweighting (\textbf{SART-rwt}), despite reaching the highest standalone detection F1 of 0.85, lags full SART by $-9.3$\,pp accuracy and $-14.0$\,pp robustness. A possible reason is that scalar reweighting acts on magnitude only: a downweighted shortcut gradient still points the wrong way, so the model simply learns the same shortcut at a slower rate. The detector's calibration (high F1) cannot compensate for keeping a harmful direction in the update. Putting the three sub-questions together: \uline{the conflict-gated projection is the single load-bearing primitive of SART, scalar reweighting on the same \textsc{ShortcutScore} fails because magnitude-only correction never changes update direction, and gate-free answer-token suppression alone collapses robustness because it neither identifies nor cancels the conflicting subspace.}

\subsection{ShortcutScore Validation and Sensitivity}
\label{sec:exp_detection}

\textbf{Detection Validation.} This experiment aims to answer: \textbf{(a) does \textsc{ShortcutScore} rank shortcut samples accurately enough to define the surgery's selection set, and (b) does the per-sample conflict gate act as a second filter that recovers still-aligned signal inside flagged-but-clean samples?}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{results/figure3.png}
    \caption{Detection-then-correction evidence chain, read left to right. \textbf{(a)} \textbf{Mechanism diagnostic}: \textsc{ShortcutScore} $S(s)$ vs.\ ground-truth shortcut rate, faint per-sample raster (gray) plus 20-bin means (blue) and a least-squares fit (red dashed)---establishes that $S(s)$ ranks samples correctly. \textbf{(b)} \textbf{Mechanism diagnostic}: alignment $A(s)$ stratified into TP/FP/TN/FN cells against the F1-optimal threshold $\tau_S^\star$---shows the per-sample conflict gate filters within the rank. \textbf{(c)} \textbf{Ablation}: clean accuracy and robustness across SART variants---the conflict-only projection is the single load-bearing primitive, and scalar reweighting on the same \textsc{ShortcutScore} fails to recover the same gain.}
    \label{fig:analysis}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Findings.}
We noticed that \textsc{ShortcutScore} reaches an F1 of 0.82 (Fig.~\ref{fig:analysis}b)---comparable to the best discrete-detector baseline---and ranks shortcut samples consistently enough to define the surgery's selection set $\mathcal{S}^\star$. The underlying driver is that the two geometric signals composing $S(s)$---non-transfer alignment and answer-gradient concentration---are themselves complementary: alignment captures direction-of-update mismatch, while concentration captures token-level credit-assignment mismatch, so together they cover both axes along which a shortcut gradient can be harmful. Another interesting observation is that the false-positive cell in panel (b)---samples flagged as shortcut by $S(s)$ but ground-truth clean---shows mean $A(s){=}+0.02$, with about $39\%$ of these still carrying positively-aligned signal at the F1-optimal threshold. A possible reason is that detection is necessarily a rank operation rather than a calibrated estimator at the per-sample level. Both halves of the question therefore split as follows: \uline{\textsc{ShortcutScore} is accurate enough as a rank to define the surgery's selection set $\mathcal{S}^\star$ at F1$=0.82$, and the per-sample conflict gate $\mathbf{g}_s^\top \mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}} < 0$ is the second filter that recovers a flagged-but-clean sample's still-aligned signal by refusing to project whenever its actual gradient has not yet turned harmful.}

\textbf{Hyperparameter sensitivity.} Under the conflict-only rule, the projection strength $\gamma$ should scale with the compositional complexity of the shortcut: $\gamma{=}0.8$ is best for single-feature Math (full projection over-erases useful residual signal), while $\gamma{=}1.0$ is best for multi-feature Financial and Causal, where the conflicting subspace must be fully removed. The answer-suppression coefficient $\rho$ is inactive unless $R(s){>}\tau_R$; it matters only on Causal, where $\rho{=}0{\sim}0.3$ both hit the top row and $\rho{=}0.5$ collapses robustness. The recommended default $(\gamma{=}1.0, \rho{=}0.3)$ degrades gracefully across the three regimes; per-task tuning adds a further $+8.2$\,pp robustness on average. Full sensitivity table and per-dataset breakdown are in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_sensitivity}.

\subsection{Real-World LLM Transfer and Computational Cost}
\label{sec:exp_llm_transfer}

\textbf{LLM Transfer.} This experiment aims to answer: \textbf{Do SART's two design pillars---gradient-geometry detection and conflict-only directional surgery---both transfer to an 8B-class language model, or does one of them degrade?}

We test SART on Qwen3-8B + LoRA rank-16 adapters on $q/k/v/o$-proj, with a controlled-injection pilot on GSM8K (70\% sum-of-numbers shortcut relabeling, tested on original-answer splits). \textbf{Findings.} We noticed that \textsc{ShortcutScore} separates injected samples (mean $S{=}0.46$) from clean samples (mean $S{=}0.007$) by more than an order of magnitude, matching the synthetic separation. The underlying driver is that the two geometric signals are properties of the gradient field, not of model scale: a gradient that fails to transfer to held-out validation behaves the same way at 3M and at 8B parameters, modulo the LoRA-subspace projection. Another interesting observation is that, on this 3-epoch LoRA budget, the surgery step does not yet produce a downstream-accuracy gain at 8B: batch-level conflict gating averages clean and shortcut gradients so the gate rarely fires on mixed batches, and answer-token suppression at $\rho{>}0$ destroys autoregressive generation (PPL rises five orders of magnitude). The two halves of the question therefore split as follows: \uline{detection transfers cleanly at 8B with the same gradient-geometry signatures as on synthetic, while surgery transfers only after two scaling adaptations are made explicit, namely per-sample conflict gating to expose the sign that batch averaging hides, and numeric-only answer-token suppression so the autoregressive generation supervision is not killed.} Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_llm_transfer} documents the full pilot at 8B.

\textbf{Computational cost.} SART completes in $\sim$60\,s per synthetic dataset on Apple M-series hardware and under 3\,min per dataset on NVIDIA H100 NVL GPUs, at $\sim$2.5$\times$ the cost of SFT. The overhead comes from per-sample gradient scoring and the surgery step; refreshing $\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}}$ every $k{=}5$ steps (once per epoch for the LLM pilot) keeps the cost amortized.
