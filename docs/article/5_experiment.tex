\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate SART on three synthetic reasoning benchmarks with controlled shortcut injection, comparing against eleven baselines spanning data-centric, loss-based, distributionally robust, and invariant learning approaches. We then transfer the method to Qwen3-8B / GSM8K under matched controlled injection.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}

\paragraph{Model and Training.} We use a GPT-style transformer (4 layers, $d_{\mathrm{model}}{=}256$, 8 heads, $d_{\mathrm{ff}}{=}1024$, ${\approx}$3.2M params) trained for 40 epochs with AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019decoupled} (lr $1{\times}10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, batch size 64, cosine annealing). SART hyperparameters ($\alpha{=}\beta{=}1.0$, $\tau_A{=}0.3$, $\tau_R{=}0.5$, $\gamma{=}1.0$, $\rho{=}0.3$, warmup $K{=}8$) are selected via an 80-configuration grid search (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_sensitivity}).

\paragraph{Datasets.} We construct three synthetic benchmarks with controlled shortcut injection: 2{,}000 train / 500 val / 1{,}000 test samples each, with training data 70\% shortcut-rule and 30\% true-rule (val/test exclusively follow the true rule; test split evenly into \emph{clean} and \emph{perturbed} by whether the shortcut agrees with the correct answer). \textbf{Math-Arithmetic}: classify $a{+}b{\geq}10$---shortcut $a{\geq}5$, true rule evaluates the sum. \textbf{Financial-Analysis}: regulatory compliance from four features---shortcut revenue${\geq}5$, true rule margin${\geq}5$ \emph{and} debt${<}5$ (multi-feature conjunction). \textbf{Causal-Reasoning}: causal-direction inference with a confounder---shortcut correlation${\geq}5$, true rule $x{\geq}5$ \emph{and} $z{<}3$ (requires confounder adjustment).

\paragraph{Baselines.} We compare against eleven methods spanning four families: \emph{loss / inference-time}---\textbf{SFT}, \textbf{Self-Consistency}~\cite{wang2023selfconsistency} (majority vote over 5 samples, $T{=}0.8$), \textbf{Focal Loss}~\cite{lin2018focallossdenseobject}, and \textbf{JTT}~\cite{liu2021justtraintwiceimproving} (upweight error-prone samples); \emph{data-centric}---\textbf{Data Filtering} (drop samples with post-warmup confidence $>0.90$) and \textbf{Influence Filtering}~\cite{koh2020understandingblackboxpredictionsinfluence}; \emph{invariance / worst-group}---\textbf{Group DRO}$^\dagger$~\cite{sagawa2020distributionallyrobustneuralnetworks}, \textbf{IRM}$^\ddagger$~\cite{arjovsky2020invariantriskminimization}, \textbf{V-REx}$^\ddagger$~\cite{krueger2021outofdistributiongeneralizationriskextrapolation}, \textbf{Fishr}$^\ddagger$~\cite{rame2022fishrinvariantgradientvariances}; and \emph{failure-mining}---\textbf{LfF}~\cite{nam2020learningfailuretrainingdebiased}. $^\dagger$ requires group annotations, $^\ddagger$ requires multiple environments.

\paragraph{Metrics.} All metrics are computed on held-out samples drawn from the true-rule distribution. \textbf{Clean Accuracy} ($\uparrow$) is token-level top-1 accuracy on the clean split. \textbf{Robustness} ($\uparrow$) is absolute accuracy on the perturbed split, where the shortcut feature \emph{contradicts} the correct label---a model that has truly learned the rule should be unaffected, a shortcut-reliant model flips. \textbf{Reasoning Consistency} ($\uparrow$) is the fraction of test examples whose greedily generated intermediate tokens exactly match the gold chain up to the separator (protocol in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_algorithm}). \textbf{Shortcut Detection F1} ($\uparrow$) is the best binary F1 over a 50-threshold sweep of each method's per-sample score---an oracle upper bound on detection quality. The ablation table additionally reports \textbf{Gradient Alignment} $|\cos(\mathbf{g}_s,\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}})|$ over a held-out subset of up to 100 samples; values near $0$ indicate updates neither systematically aligned with nor opposed to the validation-improving direction.

\textbf{Findings.}
We noticed that SART tops the per-dataset breakdown on Causal-Reasoning across every metric (accuracy, robustness, reasoning consistency), reaches the highest accuracy on Financial-Analysis, and is competitive but not first on Math-Arithmetic where the worst-group baseline Group DRO and the influence-based filter both exceed it (Table~\ref{tab:per_dataset}). The underlying driver is that gradient-based correction acts where surface filtering and worst-group reweighting cannot: on Causal, where a confounder makes shortcut and target inseparable on observable features alone, SART reaches 0.795 robustness (+10.3\,pp over the strongest baseline Influence Filtering), while filtering-based methods either over-prune the training set (Influence Filtering at 23.6\,pp robustness on Financial---worse than SFT) or are blind to multi-feature conjunctions (Group DRO at 28.8\,pp robustness on Causal). Another interesting observation is that SART's relative advantage scales with shortcut compositional complexity: against the strongest baseline per dataset, the accuracy / robustness gap moves from $-3.9$/$-8.5$\,pp on single-feature Math, to $-2.7$/$-25.4$\,pp on the conjunction-rule Financial where Data Filtering's confidence threshold catches the shortcut directly, to $+6.1$/$+10.3$\,pp on confounder-laden Causal. There are two possible reasons: 1) on single-feature shortcuts a confidence-threshold filter is already nearly optimal because the shortcut is the high-confidence head of the loss distribution, leaving little room for gradient-geometry to add; 2) on confounder-induced shortcuts, the shortcut and the target both produce confident, low-loss training examples that hard filters cannot separate, but their per-sample gradients diverge geometrically, so the conflict gate fires reliably and the projection cancels exactly the harmful direction. Thus, this experiment validates the effectiveness of gradient-geometry detection-then-correction for shortcut-aware reasoning training, with the largest gains exactly where surface-level filters fail.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:exp_ablation}

\textbf{Ablation Study.} This experiment aims to answer: \textbf{How does each component in SART impact its performance, and is a magnitude-only correction (scalar reweighting) a viable substitute for directional surgery?}

We compare SART against variants that remove or replace one component at a time: \textbf{SART-w/o-projection} disables the conflict-only projection and keeps only the answer-token suppression; \textbf{SART-w/o-suppress} sets $\rho{=}0$ and keeps only the conflict-only projection; \textbf{SART-rwt} replaces directional surgery with scalar exponential reweighting $w(s){=}\exp(-\lambda S(s))$ at $\lambda{=}3.0$, holding the detection step fixed.

\textbf{Findings.}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} shows that conflict-only projection is the load-bearing component: removing it (\textbf{-w/o-projection}) collapses robustness from 54.8 to 18.3\,pp ($-36.5$\,pp), while removing the answer-token suppression (\textbf{-w/o-suppress}, $\rho{=}0$) costs only $-3.9$\,pp robustness and $-1.1$\,pp accuracy. The underlying driver is that the conflict-gated projection is the single mechanism that converts a shortcut-flagged sample into a still-usable update, by canceling its anti-aligned direction without erasing its residual; the answer-token suppression amplifies this on tasks with token-localized shortcuts (Causal) but is not load-bearing on average. Another interesting observation is that scalar reweighting (\textbf{SART-rwt}), despite reaching the highest standalone detection F1 of 0.85, lags full SART by $-9.3$\,pp accuracy and $-14.0$\,pp robustness. A possible reason is that scalar reweighting acts on \emph{magnitude only}: a downweighted shortcut gradient still points the wrong way, so the model simply learns the same shortcut at a slower rate. The detector's calibration (high F1) cannot compensate for keeping a harmful direction in the update. Thus, this experiment validates the necessity of conditional directional correction and confirms that scalar reweighting is not a viable substitute.

\subsection{ShortcutScore Validation and Sensitivity}
\label{sec:exp_detection}

\textbf{Detection Validation.} This experiment aims to answer: \textbf{Is \textsc{ShortcutScore} a usable rank signal for the surgery's selection set, and do the surgery's hyperparameters degrade gracefully across reasoning regimes?}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{results/figure3.png}
    \caption{Empirical validation of \textsc{ShortcutScore} as a rank signal. \textbf{(a)} \textbf{Mechanism diagnostic}: \textsc{ShortcutScore} $S(s)$ vs.\ ground-truth shortcut rate, faint per-sample raster (gray) plus 20-bin means (blue) and a least-squares fit (red dashed). \textbf{(b)} \textbf{Mechanism diagnostic}: alignment $A(s)$ stratified into TP/FP/TN/FN cells against the F1-optimal threshold $\tau_S^\star$, exposing how much positively-aligned residual signal a flagged-but-clean sample (FP) still carries.}
    \label{fig:analysis}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Findings.}
We noticed that \textsc{ShortcutScore} reaches an F1 of 0.82 (Fig.~\ref{fig:analysis}b)---comparable to the best discrete-detector baseline---and ranks shortcut samples consistently enough to define the surgery's selection set $\mathcal{S}^\star$. The underlying driver is that the two geometric signals composing $S(s)$---non-transfer alignment and answer-gradient concentration---are themselves complementary: alignment captures direction-of-update mismatch, while concentration captures token-level credit-assignment mismatch, so together they cover both axes along which a shortcut gradient can be harmful. Another interesting observation is that the false-positive cell in panel (b)---samples flagged as shortcut by $S(s)$ but ground-truth clean---shows mean $A(s){=}+0.02$, with about $39\%$ of these still carrying positively-aligned signal at the F1-optimal threshold. A possible reason is that detection is necessarily a rank operation rather than a calibrated estimator at the per-sample level; this is precisely why directional surgery, gated on per-sample $\mathbf{g}_s^\top \mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}} < 0$ rather than on detection alone, is the right correction primitive: the gate refuses to project on a flagged sample whose actual gradient has not yet turned harmful, recovering the false positives at the surgery step.

\textbf{Hyperparameter sensitivity.} Under the conflict-only rule, the projection strength $\gamma$ should scale with the compositional complexity of the shortcut: $\gamma{=}0.8$ is best for single-feature Math (full projection over-erases useful residual signal), while $\gamma{=}1.0$ is best for multi-feature Financial and Causal, where the conflicting subspace must be fully removed. The answer-suppression coefficient $\rho$ is inactive unless $R(s){>}\tau_R$; it matters only on Causal, where $\rho{=}0{\sim}0.3$ both hit the top row and $\rho{=}0.5$ collapses robustness. The recommended default $(\gamma{=}1.0, \rho{=}0.3)$ degrades gracefully across the three regimes; per-task tuning adds a further $+8.2$\,pp robustness on average. Full sensitivity table and per-dataset breakdown are in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_sensitivity}.

\subsection{Real-World LLM Transfer and Computational Cost}
\label{sec:exp_llm_transfer}

\textbf{LLM Transfer.} This experiment aims to answer: \textbf{Does the gradient-geometry detection signal transfer to an 8B-class language model under matched controlled shortcut injection, and what scaling work is needed for the correction step?}

We test SART on Qwen3-8B + LoRA rank-16 adapters on $q/k/v/o$-proj, with a controlled-injection pilot on GSM8K (70\% sum-of-numbers shortcut relabeling, tested on original-answer splits). \textbf{Findings.} We noticed that \textsc{ShortcutScore} separates injected samples (mean $S{=}0.46$) from clean samples (mean $S{=}0.007$) by more than an order of magnitude, matching the synthetic separation. The underlying driver is that the two geometric signals are properties of the gradient field, not of model scale: a gradient that fails to transfer to held-out validation behaves the same way at 3M and at 8B parameters, modulo the LoRA-subspace projection. Another interesting observation is that, on this 3-epoch LoRA budget, the surgery step does not yet produce a downstream-accuracy gain at 8B: batch-level conflict gating averages clean and shortcut gradients so the gate rarely fires on mixed batches, and answer-token suppression at $\rho{>}0$ destroys autoregressive generation (PPL rises five orders of magnitude). A possible reason is that scaling the surgery to LLMs requires (i) per-sample rather than batch-level conflict gating to expose the per-sample sign, and (ii) restricting answer-token suppression to numeric answer tokens so generation tokens are not suppressed; both directions are concrete follow-ups that do not change the formulation. Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_llm_transfer} documents the full pilot at 8B.

\textbf{Computational cost.} SART completes in $\sim$60\,s per synthetic dataset on Apple M-series hardware and under 3\,min per dataset on NVIDIA H100 NVL GPUs, at $\sim$2.5$\times$ the cost of SFT. The overhead comes from per-sample gradient scoring and the surgery step; refreshing $\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}}$ every $k{=}5$ steps (once per epoch for the LLM pilot) keeps the cost amortized.
