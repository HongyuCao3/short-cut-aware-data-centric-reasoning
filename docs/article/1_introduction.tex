\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) deployed in high-stakes reasoning tasks frequently exploit \textit{reasoning shortcuts}---surface pattern matching, answer memorization, keyword-answer correlations, and premature answer prediction---rather than performing genuine logical inference~\cite{wei2022chain,wang2022self}. This shortcut reliance stems from \textbf{training signal misalignment}: shortcut-encouraging samples efficiently reduce training loss but fail to foster generalizable reasoning. Consequently, models accurate on training distributions fail catastrophically when numbers change, constraints vary, or problem phrasing shifts. This motivates \textit{\textbf{S}hortcut-\textbf{A}ware \textbf{R}easoning \textbf{T}raining (SART)}: identifying samples that promote shortcut updates and modifying gradient dynamics so that genuine reasoning signals dominate. Solving SART is critical for reliable LLM deployment in mathematical reasoning, financial analysis, planning, and scientific discovery---domains where spurious reasoning leads to incorrect decisions or unsafe recommendations.

Two main challenges arise in solving SART: 1) detecting shortcut-promoting samples from gradient behavior; 2) modifying training dynamics to suppress shortcut learning without discarding samples or destabilizing training. Firstly, shortcut samples are often correctly labeled and indistinguishable by conventional quality metrics. They produce strong gradients that efficiently reduce training loss, making loss-based detection unreliable. The detection challenge seeks to answer: how can we identify training samples whose gradients improve loss but harm reasoning generalization? Secondly, simply down-weighting or removing detected shortcut samples is insufficient---a shortcut-flagged sample typically still carries a useful, validation-aligned gradient component that hard removal or scalar reweighting silently destroys. The suppression challenge aims to answer: how can we cancel only the harmful directional component of a sample's gradient while preserving the rest?

Prior literature partially addresses these two challenges along three lines.\\
\textbf{1) Inference-time and loss-shaping methods}~\cite{wei2022chain,kojima2022large,wang2022self,lin2018focallossdenseobject,liu2021justtraintwiceimproving,nam2020learningfailuretrainingdebiased}, in which Chain-of-Thought training, Self-Consistency decoding, focal-loss reweighting, and failure-mining objectives operate either at decoding time or on scalar loss values.
However, \textbf{shortcut samples and legitimately-hard samples are scalar-loss-indistinguishable}, and inference-time decoding leaves the shortcut-encoding parameters intact.\\
\textbf{2) Annotation-driven invariance methods}~\cite{sagawa2020distributionallyrobustneuralnetworks,arjovsky2020invariantriskminimization,krueger2021outofdistributiongeneralizationriskextrapolation,rame2022fishrinvariantgradientvariances}, in which Group DRO, IRM, V-REx, and Fishr enforce worst-group or cross-environment invariance.
However, \textbf{they require shortcut-group labels or environment partitions that practitioners rarely possess on real reasoning corpora}.\\
\textbf{3) Sample-removal methods}~\cite{bengio2009curriculum,koh2020understandingblackboxpredictionsinfluence}, in which data filtering and influence-function selection drop high-confidence or high-influence samples.
However, \textbf{a single sample's gradient mixes harmful and useful directions, and removing the entire sample destroys the useful component along with the shortcut}.
Existing studies show the inability to jointly address detection and per-sample directional correction. Thus, we need a novel perspective to derive a novel formulation for shortcut-aware reasoning training.

\textbf{Our Contribution: A Gradient-Geometry Detection-then-Correction Perspective.} To fundamentally fill these gaps, we formulate the shortcut-aware reasoning training problem as a gradient-geometry detection-then-correction task and propose a \textbf{S}hortcut-\textbf{A}ware \textbf{R}easoning \textbf{T}raining framework, namely \textbf{SART}. To advance detection precision and directional correction, this framework implements two steps: 1) gradient-structure shortcut detection; 2) conflict-only gradient surgery. Step 1 is to flag shortcut-promoting samples without relying on labels. Specifically, we compute a per-sample gradient and a small validation gradient $\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}}$, and combine non-transfer alignment with answer-token concentration into a unified \textbf{ShortcutScore} $S(s)$ that ranks samples by shortcut tendency. The key insight is that \textbf{shortcut samples carry a distinctive gradient signature---low cosine alignment with $\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}}$ and gradient energy concentrated on answer tokens---that is observable from training dynamics alone, with no shortcut labels or environment splits}. Step 2 is to neutralize harmful directions inside flagged samples while preserving their validation-aligned components. Specifically, for each flagged sample we apply a PCGrad-style~\cite{yu2020gradient} conflict-only projection that subtracts the component opposing $\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}}$ only when the per-sample gradient genuinely conflicts with it, and a complementary answer-token suppression term where appropriate. The key insight is that \textbf{detection alone identifies which samples need intervention, but only conditional directional correction can extract their useful signal without discarding it---hard removal and scalar reweighting both fail this test, whereas conflict-only projection is precisely the minimal operation that satisfies it}. Finally, we present extensive experiments and case studies to show the effectiveness and robustness of our framework, including a controlled-injection pilot on Qwen3-8B / GSM8K that confirms the detection geometry transfers to an 8B-class language model.
