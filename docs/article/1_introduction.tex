\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) achieve strong performance on reasoning benchmarks~\cite{wei2022chain,wang2022self,kojima2022large}, yet often rely on shortcuts rather than on genuine reasoning. They exploit surface patterns, memorize answer templates, and latch onto spurious input-output correlations~\cite{geirhos2020shortcut,mccoy2019right,niven2019probing}. These behaviors reduce training loss efficiently but collapse under even mild distribution shifts: when numbers change, problems are rephrased, or confounders are introduced, accuracy drops sharply. The gap reveals a fundamental issue: current training objectives optimize answer correctness but do not enforce \emph{how} the model arrives at that answer. As a result, models learn to produce the right answers for the wrong reasons.

A central question follows: where, during training, does the distinction between reasoning and shortcut actually appear? Loss alone cannot tell them apart---shortcut and reasoning samples often yield identical loss values. Data annotations are rarely available to mark shortcut behavior on real corpora. We argue that the distinction emerges not in the data or in the scalar loss, but in the \emph{geometry of gradients}. A per-sample gradient encodes how that sample updates the model: shortcut samples may reduce loss, yet their gradient directions fail to improve generalization, while reasoning-consistent samples produce updates aligned with held-out validation improvement. Reasoning versus shortcut is therefore more directly exposed by gradient direction than by data labels or scalar loss alone.

Building on this view, we formulate \emph{Shortcut-Aware Reasoning Training} (SART) as the problem of identifying and correcting harmful gradient directions during training. Two challenges arise. First, \textbf{detection}: shortcut-promoting samples are typically correctly labeled and indistinguishable under standard quality or loss-based metrics, yet their gradients do not transfer to validation performance. Second, \textbf{correction}: even when a sample is identified as shortcut-prone, its gradient is not purely harmful---it mixes useful and harmful components. Hard removal~\cite{koh2020understandingblackboxpredictionsinfluence} discards the useful component along with the harmful, while scalar reweighting~\cite{lin2018focallossdenseobject,liu2021justtraintwiceimproving} leaves the harmful direction unchanged at lower magnitude. The problem is therefore not whether to use a sample, but which directions of its gradient should be trusted.

We address both challenges through a gradient-geometry detection-and-correction framework. The key idea is to decompose each per-sample update into components aligned and misaligned with a small validation gradient $\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}}$ that represents generalizable reasoning. We introduce \textsc{ShortcutScore}, which ranks shortcut-prone samples using two complementary signals: low alignment with $\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}}$, indicating poor transfer to generalization, and high concentration of gradient energy on answer tokens, indicating reliance on answer prediction rather than on intermediate reasoning. We then apply a per-sample, conflict-only projection that removes only the component of a flagged sample's gradient conflicting with $\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}}$; its closed form matches a PCGrad-style update~\cite{yu2020gradient} but is derived here as a minimal feasible correction to the per-sample gradient. The resulting intervention is minimal and targeted: rather than suppressing entire samples, we reshape updates at the level of gradient direction.

Our contributions are fivefold. \textbf{(1) A new formulation:} shortcut behavior is a gradient-alignment phenomenon, observable from training dynamics rather than from data labels or scalar loss. \textbf{(2) A decomposition:} each training sample carries both validation-aligned and conflicting gradient components, ruling out sample-level dropping or magnitude rescaling as the right primitive. \textbf{(3) A principle:} directional correction---acting on which gradient directions to trust rather than which samples to keep---is the empirically load-bearing mechanism for removing shortcut signals without destroying useful information. \textbf{(4) An instantiation:} we propose SART, which operationalizes this principle through a \textsc{ShortcutScore} detector and a per-sample, conflict-only projection, improving accuracy by $+4.7$\,pp and robustness by $+7.1$\,pp over the strongest of eleven baselines~\cite{sagawa2020distributionallyrobustneuralnetworks,arjovsky2020invariantriskminimization} on three controlled-injection benchmarks. \textbf{(5) A scaling insight:} on a Qwen3-8B / GSM8K pilot, detection transfers cleanly across scale, while correction must respect autoregressive generation constraints---a constrained variant of the same gradient-alignment principle. Together, these contributions reframe shortcut mitigation from data filtering or loss shaping into directional inference over the per-sample gradient field.

\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) achieve strong performance on reasoning benchmarks through instruction tuning and chain-of-thought supervision~\cite{wei2022chain,wang2022self,kojima2022large}. However, these gains are often structurally fragile. Models can answer correctly by exploiting superficial cues, memorized answer patterns, or spurious input-output correlations rather than by performing genuine reasoning~\cite{geirhos2020shortcut,mccoy2019right,niven2019probing}. As a result, small perturbations---changing numbers, reordering options, or paraphrasing the same question---can cause large performance drops despite preserving semantics.

We identify three recurring failure modes in shortcut-prone reasoning training. First, \textbf{loss ambiguity}: shortcut-promoting samples and reasoning-consistent samples often produce nearly identical scalar losses, making them indistinguishable to standard optimization objectives. Second, \textbf{sample ambiguity}: a single training example can simultaneously contain useful reasoning signal and harmful shortcut signal. Third, \textbf{correction ambiguity}: existing interventions such as filtering, reweighting, or robust optimization modify which samples are used or how strongly they are weighted, but do not explicitly control which gradient directions are trusted. As a result, harmful shortcut directions remain embedded within the update itself.

\textbf{Our Perspective.} We argue that these failures arise from a common structural issue: existing shortcut mitigation methods treat each sample update as a monolithic optimization signal, without distinguishing between gradient components that improve generalizable reasoning and components that merely exploit shortcuts. Consequently, shortcut-promoting directions are propagated into training together with useful reasoning information. The distinction between reasoning and shortcut therefore appears not primarily in the data or scalar loss, but in the geometry of per-sample gradients. Gradients aligned with held-out validation improvement tend to encode transferable reasoning behavior, while shortcut-promoting gradients reduce training loss without improving generalization.

The fix requires two components organized around one geometric principle: (i) identify which parts of a sample gradient are inconsistent with generalizable reasoning, and (ii) suppress only those conflicting components while preserving the aligned residual. This changes the intervention primitive. The goal is not to decide which samples to keep, but which directions of each sample's gradient should be trusted.

\textbf{Proposed Framework.} We introduce \emph{Shortcut-Aware Reasoning Training} (SART), a gradient-geometry framework for shortcut detection and correction. First, SART introduces \textsc{ShortcutScore}, which identifies shortcut-prone updates using two complementary signals: low alignment with a small validation gradient and high concentration of gradient energy on final-answer tokens rather than intermediate reasoning tokens. Second, SART applies a per-sample, conflict-only projection that removes only the component of a flagged gradient conflicting with the validation direction, while preserving its aligned component. Unlike hard filtering or scalar reweighting, SART reshapes the update itself at the level where shortcut behavior appears.

To evaluate shortcut mitigation beyond average accuracy, we introduce a controlled shortcut evaluation protocol that measures performance under shortcut perturbations. Across three controlled reasoning benchmarks, SART consistently improves over prior shortcut mitigation methods while substantially improving robustness under shortcut injection. Ablation studies further show that directional correction, rather than shortcut detection alone, is the empirically load-bearing mechanism.

\textbf{Contributions.} i) We identify three fragility sources in shortcut-prone reasoning training: loss ambiguity, sample ambiguity, and correction ambiguity, and show that they arise from a common failure to distinguish aligned and conflicting components of per-sample gradients.
ii) We propose SART that detects shortcut-promoting updates through gradient geometry and corrects them through per-sample conflict-only projection.
iii) We introduce a robustness-oriented evaluation protocol for shortcut-aware reasoning that complements standard accuracy and isolates the causal role of directional correction under controlled shortcut injection.
iv) Across three reasoning benchmarks, SART improves over the strongest of eleven baselines by $+4.7$\,pp accuracy and $+7.1$\,pp robustness, while ablations show that removing conflict-only projection reduces robustness by $36.5$\,pp.
v) A Qwen3-8B/GSM8K pilot further shows that the detection geometry transfers to pretrained LLMs, while full correction at this scale must respect autoregressive generation constraints.

Together, these results suggest that shortcut-aware reasoning does not lack useful training signal; it lacks directional discrimination. SART addresses this gap by operating directly on the geometry of the per-sample gradient field.
