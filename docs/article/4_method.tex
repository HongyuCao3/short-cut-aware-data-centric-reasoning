\subsection{Detection: \textsc{ShortcutScore} from Gradient Geometry}
\label{sec:method_detection}

Because shortcut samples are typically correctly labeled and produce strong, low-loss gradients, loss- and label-based filters cannot separate them from legitimately informative samples. We instead detect shortcut-promoting samples from the geometry of their gradients, using two complementary signals that cover both axes along which a shortcut gradient can be harmful.

\textbf{Non-Transfer Gradient Alignment.} For each training sample $s = (x, y)$ we compute the per-sample gradient $\mathbf{g}_s = \nabla_\theta \ell(\theta; s)$ and the validation gradient
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}} = \nabla_\theta \frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|} \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \ell(\theta; v),
\end{equation}
and define their non-transfer alignment as
\begin{equation}
    A(s) = \cos(\mathbf{g}_s,\, \mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}}) = \frac{\mathbf{g}_s^\top \mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}}}{\|\mathbf{g}_s\|\|\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}}\|}.
\end{equation}
A low value of $A(s)$ indicates that $s$ pulls the parameters in a direction inconsistent with improving validation reasoning---a hallmark of shortcut exploitation. Geometrically, $A(s)$ is the angle between the direction in which $s$ updates the model and the direction in which held-out reasoning quality grows.

\textbf{Answer-Gradient Concentration.} Let $T_{\mathrm{ans}}$ and $T_{\mathrm{reason}}$ denote the index sets of answer tokens and reasoning tokens in the output sequence. The answer-gradient concentration is
\begin{equation}
    R(s) = \frac{\sum_{t \in T_{\mathrm{ans}}} \|\nabla_\theta \ell_t\|}{\sum_{t \in T_{\mathrm{ans}} \cup T_{\mathrm{reason}}} \|\nabla_\theta \ell_t\|},
\end{equation}
where $\ell_t$ is the token-level loss at position $t$. A high $R(s)$ indicates updates dominated by answer prediction rather than by intermediate reasoning steps: the model learns \emph{what to answer} rather than \emph{how to reason}. When the answer/reasoning split is unavailable (answer-only supervision, $T_{\mathrm{reason}}=\emptyset$), we set $R(s)=\tau_R$ so the second score term vanishes and \textsc{ShortcutScore} degrades gracefully to an alignment-only detector (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_graceful}).

\textbf{\textsc{ShortcutScore} and Selection Set.} The two signals combine into a single rank,
\begin{equation}
    S(s) = \alpha \cdot \max(0,\, \tau_A - A(s)) + \beta \cdot \max(0,\, R(s) - \tau_R),
\end{equation}
in which $\tau_A,\tau_R$ are thresholds and $\alpha,\beta$ are balancing hyperparameters. Samples with high $S(s)$ enter the selection set
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{S}^\star = \{\, s \in \mathcal{D} : S(s) > \tau_S^\star \,\},
\end{equation}
where $\tau_S^\star$ is set to the F1-optimal threshold over a held-out probe. Samples in $\mathcal{S}^\star$ enter the gradient-surgery step (\S\ref{sec:gradient_surgery}); samples outside $\mathcal{S}^\star$ contribute their gradient unchanged. The validation gradient $\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}}$ is recomputed periodically to keep the alignment signal current without per-step overhead.

The detector is a rank, not a calibrated estimator. It only needs to order samples well enough that the downstream conflict gate has the right working set; the gate itself absorbs rank-level false positives by refusing to project whenever a flagged sample's gradient has not yet turned harmful. Pseudocode appears in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_algorithm}.

\subsection{Correction: Conflict-Only Gradient Surgery}
\label{sec:gradient_surgery}

Detection identifies which samples need intervention; correction modifies their gradients. Following the opener, we want a correction that acts on direction rather than magnitude and only when there is a genuine conflict to resolve.

\textbf{Minimal Directional Correction.} For each flagged sample $s \in \mathcal{S}^\star$, we seek the smallest modification of $\mathbf{g}_s$ that removes its conflict with the validation-improving direction:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:projection_objective}
    \min_{\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}_s} \;\;
    \|\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}_s - \mathbf{g}_s\|_2^2
    \quad \text{s.t.} \quad
    \widetilde{\mathbf{g}}_s^\top \mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}} \ge 0.
\end{equation}
This objective makes the correction principle explicit: preserve as much of the original update as possible subject to non-conflict with $\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}}$. Its closed-form solution is the Euclidean projection onto the half-space $\{\widetilde{\mathbf{g}} : \widetilde{\mathbf{g}}^\top \mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}} \geq 0\}$, applied with a strength parameter $\gamma \in [0,1]$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gradient_surgery}
    \mathbf{g}_s' =
    \begin{cases}
        \mathbf{g}_s - \gamma\,\dfrac{\mathbf{g}_s^\top \mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}}}{\|\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}}\|^2}\,\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}}, & \mathbf{g}_s^\top \mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}} < 0, \\[4pt]
        \mathbf{g}_s, & \text{otherwise.}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
This rule coincides with PCGrad-style~\cite{yu2020gradient} conflict removal but is derived here as the minimal feasible correction to the per-sample update. It is deliberately asymmetric: even a flagged sample may carry a useful aligned component, which should be preserved; projection fires only when the gradient genuinely opposes validation improvement.

\textbf{Answer-Token Suppression.} For $s \in \mathcal{S}^\star$ with $R(s) > \tau_R$, we further decompose $\mathbf{g}_s'$ into answer-token and reasoning-token components and suppress the answer-dominant part:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{g}_s'' = (1 - \rho)\,\mathbf{g}_s'^{\,\mathrm{ans}} + \mathbf{g}_s'^{\,\mathrm{reason}},
\end{equation}
where $\rho \in [0,1]$ is the suppression coefficient. Setting $\rho=0$ disables this term and recovers pure conflict-only projection; setting $\rho=1$ removes the answer-token gradient entirely. In practice $\rho>0$ is beneficial for tasks with token-localized shortcuts but may harm autoregressive generation in pretrained LLMs, so we default to $\rho=0$ in that regime.

\textbf{Combined Update Rule.} The batch gradient is
\[
\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{batch}} =
\sum_{s \in \mathcal{B},\, s \in \mathcal{S}^\star} \mathbf{g}_s'' \;+\;
\sum_{s \in \mathcal{B},\, s \notin \mathcal{S}^\star} \mathbf{g}_s,
\]
fed to a standard optimizer (e.g., AdamW). The validation gradient $\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}}$ is refreshed every $k$ steps so the projection direction remains current.

\paragraph{Detection--Correction symmetry.} Detection determines \emph{which samples} require intervention; correction determines \emph{which directions} within those samples should be retained. Removing either layer weakens the method: detection without correction passes harmful directions to the optimizer unchanged, while correction without detection erases useful signal from clean samples.

\paragraph{Scaling to pretrained LLMs.} A naive implementation requires storing dense per-sample gradients, which is infeasible for large models. We address this through three orthogonal mechanisms. First, we restrict optimization to a low-dimensional trainable subspace such as LoRA adapters~\cite{hu2022lora}, reducing gradient size without altering the formulation. Second, we optionally use random sketches that preserve inner products under the Johnson--Lindenstrauss lemma, so cosine alignment $A(s)$ is approximated within $\varepsilon$-error during scoring. Third, we update $\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{V}}$ periodically rather than every step. These approximations preserve the alignment geometry while making SART practical at scale; any inner-product-preserving proxy (Fisher-diagonal, last-layer gradients, influence approximations~\cite{koh2020understandingblackboxpredictionsinfluence}) can be slotted in. Full derivations appear in Appendix~\ref{sec:scalable_gradients}.
