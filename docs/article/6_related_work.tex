\section{Related Work}

\textbf{LLM training and inference-time reasoning.}
Supervised fine-tuning and RLHF optimize models toward task-correct or human-preferred outputs~\cite{ouyang2022training}, while Chain-of-Thought prompting~\cite{wei2022chain,kojima2022large} and Self-Consistency decoding~\cite{wang2022self} improve reasoning by eliciting or aggregating intermediate traces at inference time. These methods improve answer quality, but do not inspect the training updates through which shortcut behavior is encoded. SART is complementary: it acts not on prompts, decoding, or preference objectives, but on the per-sample gradient directions that shape adaptation.

\textbf{Shortcut learning and invariance.}
Shortcut behavior has been documented in vision~\cite{geirhos2020shortcut}, natural language inference~\cite{mccoy2019right}, and argument reasoning~\cite{niven2019probing}. Mitigation methods often require group labels or environment partitions, as in Group DRO~\cite{sagawa2020distributionallyrobustneuralnetworks}, IRM~\cite{arjovsky2020invariantriskminimization}, V-REx~\cite{krueger2021outofdistributiongeneralizationriskextrapolation}, and Fishr~\cite{rame2022fishrinvariantgradientvariances}, or infer bias from scalar training behavior, as in JTT~\cite{liu2021justtraintwiceimproving} and LfF~\cite{nam2020learningfailuretrainingdebiased}. Real reasoning corpora rarely provide shortcut labels or environments, and scalar loss cannot reliably separate shortcut examples from legitimately hard ones. SART instead defines shortcut behavior dynamically through validation-gradient misalignment.

\textbf{Validation-driven sample correction.}
Meta-reweighting learns scalar example weights through a validation objective~\cite{ren2018learning}, and influence-function methods estimate which samples most affect held-out loss~\cite{koh2020understandingblackboxpredictionsinfluence}. These methods introduce the right validation-driven principle, but retain a coarse action space: a sample is upweighted, downweighted, or removed as a whole. This is insufficient when one reasoning example contains both useful reasoning signal and shortcut-promoting signal. SART keeps the validation-driven view but changes the intervention primitive from sample-level magnitude control to direction-level correction.

\textbf{Gradient surgery and projection.}
Gradient projection has long been used in constrained optimization~\cite{rosen1960gradient} and modern learning settings such as trust-region optimization~\cite{schulman2015trust}, continual learning~\cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming}, and multi-task conflict resolution. PCGrad~\cite{yu2020gradient} removes conflicting components between task gradients. SART inherits the conflict-only projection idea but applies it to a different object: the conflict between a per-sample training gradient and a validation gradient representing transferable reasoning. The projection is conditional, label-free, and sample-local.

\textbf{Positioning.}
Prior work improves reasoning outputs, identifies biased samples or groups, assigns scalar importance to examples, or resolves task-level gradient conflicts. SART occupies a different point in this design space: shortcut mitigation is not about which samples to keep, but which directions within each sample update should be trusted.
